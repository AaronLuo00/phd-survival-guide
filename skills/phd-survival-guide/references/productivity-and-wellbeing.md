## Goals and output

- **North star**: long-term vision of the impact you want → a 3-year plan with yearly milestones → quarterly deliverables (papers, code, experiments) → weekly targets.
- **Output-driven**: aim for 2–3 quality papers a year, reusable research tools, regular talks at lab meetings and conferences, and 1–2 active collaborations.
- **Tracking system** (e.g., Notion): research dashboard, literature database, experiment log, idea capture, weekly review.

## Daily routine and mode switching

- **Morning**: 2–4 hours of deep work on the most important task; no email or meetings.
- **Afternoon**: meetings, discussions, code review, email and admin, paper reading.
- **Evening**: log progress, plan tomorrow's priorities, light reading.

Research life switches between three modes: **deep production** (writing, implementing), **wide reading** (skimming, noting trends), and **collaborative engagement** (meetings, managing projects). Each needs a different mindset. Schedule reading in low-energy afternoons, cluster deep work in the morning, and respect the real cost of context switching.

## Time management: the 80/20 rule

Spend about 80% of your time on core research that feeds papers, on writing and communication, and on deep expertise in your area. Limit talks outside your area, admin, and tangents to about 20%.

## The planning fallacy

We systematically underestimate how long tasks take, even after repeated delays, because we picture the ideal path, underestimate surprises, overestimate control, and remember good weeks more vividly than bad ones. External pressure (advisor meetings, deadlines, wanting to look productive) and internal drives (excitement, fear of disappointing people, wanting to prove yourself) make it worse.

**Strategies**
1. **Ruthless decomposition**: break work into the smallest meaningful units with time estimates, then multiply by 1.5–2×.
2. **Estimate vs. actual log**: track a few weeks to find your personal multiplier (the author's was about 2.3×; e.g., literature review 4h → 9h, debugging 2h → 7h, writing methods 6h → 12h).
3. **Defensive scheduling**: commit only what fits in 60% of your available time; schedule buffer blocks; keep an optimistic internal timeline separate from the timeline you promise others.

Over-promising isn't ambition; it's being unrealistic. Real ambition is sustainable progress built on honest self-knowledge.

## Rest

Guilt when resting often comes from tying self-worth to output, a conditioned "progress = safety" response, or avoiding feelings that surface when still. **Rest is not a reward for good behavior; it is the prerequisite for it.** Real rest restores energy, incubates ideas (breakthroughs often come on walks, in the shower, during sleep), and keeps a steady rhythm instead of push-and-collapse.

| Old script | Updated belief |
| --- | --- |
| "I can't rest yet" | High-quality rest is readiness for tomorrow's work |
| "I'll fall behind" | Recovery helps me go further |
| "Everyone else is grinding" | Others are sprinting; I'm training for a marathon |
| "I haven't earned rest" | Rest is strategy, not luxury |

Sustainable basics: take weekends off and use vacation; exercise 3–4 times a week; keep relationships and hobbies outside research; build a support network of peers and mentors. Exhausted researchers make poor decisions and write buggy code.

## Imposter syndrome

Very common, especially after conferences, reading impressive papers, or joining a new lab. Signs: "I don't belong here," crediting success to luck, fear of being "found out," constant comparison.

Coping: keep a wins journal; remember others don't advertise their struggles; compare yourself with your past self; talk to peers; reframe ("I don't know enough" → "I'm here to learn"; "That was luck" → "I created the conditions for success"; "Everyone is smarter" → "Everyone has different strengths").

## Body as infrastructure, not a reward

A PhD removes most outside structure: deadlines are far apart, feedback takes months, nobody checks whether you slept. The old engine of fear, comparison, and self-criticism can win exams but tends to burn out over five years.

- **Fix the wake-up time first.** A consistent wake time plus 10–30 minutes of outdoor morning light repairs a drifting schedule better than forcing an early bedtime.
- **No big decisions late at night or on bad days.** Decide when calm; on bad days, only execute what you already decided.
- **Move most days, for reasons you get today**: something you'd enjoy even without a weight or appearance goal. On low-drive evenings, exercising first often brings drive back. "Nobody will like me until I look different" is the old exam engine in gym clothes.
- **Judge leisure by how you feel afterward.** Recovered and ready is good rest; a binge that leaves you exhausted and up at 3 a.m. is not.
- **Treat health admin as real work**: checkups, prescriptions, dentist on the calendar.

## Four foundations and the low-day protocol

Four foundations of a good working state, bottom up: **energy** (sleep, food, movement, health) → **direction** (clear plan, concrete next step) → **drive** (a mindset that lets you start without self-attack) → **environment and feedback** (a good place to work, people who see your output). When stuck, check from the bottom: energy first.

**Minimum protocol for low days** (don't ask whether you feel like it; don't re-evaluate your life):
- Outside in daylight for 10–30 minutes
- One normal meal and a shower
- Open the main project for 5–25 minutes and do one small, concrete action
- At night, write what happened: facts only, no grading

Rules: below 60 points, no scoring, only rescue. When self-criticism shows up, make the task smaller, not bigger. Within five minutes of drifting, do one action that brings you back.

## Friends and what to tell whom

Alone with slow feedback, your inner critic becomes your only reviewer. Friends remind you that you're a person, not a progress bar.

- One standing non-work plan every week (meal, sport, walk), on the calendar.
- At least one friend you can tell the truth to. "I've been pretty low lately" is a complete sentence.
- Friends outside your lab and field.
- Work near people: office, library, co-working.
- Don't wait until you've recovered to reach out.

| Who | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| Most people | Nothing, or "I've been adjusting lately." No explanation owed. |
| Close friends | The truth, briefly. It usually brings you closer. |
| Advisor and collaborators | Your state in one line, then the plan. Details and labels optional. |

**Explanation vs. excuse.** An excuse stops at the state ("I was in bad shape, so it didn't get done"). An explanation continues to ownership ("I was in bad shape and progress slipped. I'm adjusting and getting support. Next, I'll deliver X by Y.").

Template for an advisor:
> "I haven't been in great shape these past few weeks, and progress has been slower than planned—sorry about that. I'm actively working on it, including getting support through campus counseling. My next step is X, and I'll show you Y by [date]."

What rebuilds trust is the small promises kept afterward, not the wording.

## Talking to a professional

Seeing a counselor isn't a last resort and says nothing bad about you; it's like seeing a doctor when an injury doesn't heal on its own. Most universities make it free or low-cost for students, and starting early usually shortens the hard stretch.

**Signs it's worth booking a conversation:** low mood, flatness, or loss of interest for more than two weeks; sleep that keeps drifting, or mornings that feel worst; comparing yourself with peers until you feel hopeless, or thinking "I'm just pretending so I can be lazy"; months of insight with little change in behavior; having thought "maybe I should talk to someone" more than twice.

| The thought | Closer to true |
| --- | --- |
| "I need to figure out why first." | Recovery doesn't require a full explanation; causes can be worked out with a counselor. |
| "My environment is great; I have no reason to feel this way." | Low mood doesn't check your circumstances, any more than the flu does. |
| "I'm making excuses, or faking it." | People who are simply lazy rarely interrogate themselves this hard; that doubt is a common part of feeling low. |
| "I'll reach out once I'm a bit better." | The most common delay. Reaching out is part of getting better. |
| "It's awkward to go back to my old therapist." | Clinicians see returning clients all the time. "I'd like to start again" is enough. |

**How to start (US universities):**
1. Find the counseling center; usually covered by the student health fee. Harvard example: CAMHS (https://camhs.huhs.harvard.edu/) is free for students and starts with a 20-minute phone consultation at (617) 495-2042.
2. Say briefly what's going on; notes are fine.
3. Ask about longer-term care; campus centers often work short-term and refer to outside therapists covered by student insurance.
4. Switch clinicians if it isn't a fit; that's normal.
5. Medication questions go to a psychiatrist or other prescriber.

Helping the person take the first step (for example, drafting a short email or call script to their counseling center) is often the most useful thing to offer.

## Crisis resources

If someone mentions thoughts of ending their life or hurting themselves: in the US, call or text **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, https://988lifeline.org/); Harvard students can also call the 24/7 CAMHS Cares line at **(617) 495-2042**; elsewhere, the local emergency number. Encourage them to tell someone they trust right away.
