## Why these skills matter

The habits built now pay off when presenting at international conferences (one shot to impress), defending a thesis, interviewing, leading a team, and pitching to investors or stakeholders. Clear communication, rigorous habits, good documentation, and collaboration make you trusted and valued anywhere.

## Skills to develop

- Programming: Python, PyTorch/TensorFlow, Git
- Mathematics: linear algebra, probability, optimization
- Writing: technical writing, LaTeX
- Presentation: public speaking, visualization

## Conference networking

**Before**
- Review accepted papers and pick authors you want to meet.
- Prepare a 30-second pitch of your research.
- Schedule meetings in advance; join the conference Slack/Discord.

**During**
- Poster sessions are the easiest place to meet people.
- Ask thoughtful questions in Q&A.
- Go to socials and workshops.
- Sit with strangers at lunch, not just labmates; linger at coffee breaks.
- Exchange contact information.

**After**
- Follow up within 48 hours while memory is fresh.
- Share relevant papers or resources; look for collaboration opportunities.
- Stay connected; add contacts to a network spreadsheet.

**Conversation starters**
- "What brings you to this conference?"
- "I really enjoyed your talk/poster on X. Have you considered Y?"
- "Which talks are you most excited about?"
- "How does your work relate to this session's topic?"
- "I'm working on something similar—could I ask your thoughts on X?"

## Tips for introverts

- Small goals: 2–3 new people a day, not 20.
- Buddy system with a more outgoing colleague.
- Volunteer or chair a session; a role makes conversations easier.
- Prefer one-on-one coffee chats to big groups.
- Step outside to recharge between sessions.

## Online presence

Core platforms: Google Scholar (keep publications current), X/Twitter (share papers, join discussions), LinkedIn (professional updates, jobs), GitHub (code), a personal website (portfolio and research statement), Handshake for internships.

Focus on 2–3 platforms. Sustainable cadence: LinkedIn 1–2 posts a month, X 1–2 a week, website monthly, GitHub when releasing code. Consistency beats frequency.

**What to share:** your papers with a 1–2 sentence summary, interesting papers from others, conference takeaways, milestones, thoughtful comments on trending topics. Roughly 80% others' work and insights, 20% self-promotion.

**Quick start:** pick 2 platforms; same professional photo everywhere; a clear bio ("PhD student at [University] working on [specific topic]"); follow 20–30 key researchers; set a weekly reminder to post or engage; comment before connecting. Start small, be authentic, engage meaningfully, stay consistent.

## Academic website

Your site is often the first search result for your name: it controls your narrative, showcases work beyond papers, supports job applications, and links your other profiles.

**Templates (free on GitHub Pages):**
- Hugo Academic: https://github.com/wowchemy/starter-hugo-academic (most popular, built-in publications)
- al-folio: https://github.com/alshedivat/al-folio (minimal, elegant, widely used in CS)
- AcademicPages: https://github.com/academicpages/academicpages.github.io (simple, good for beginners)
- Jekyll themes: Minimal Mistakes, Modern Resume, Minima, Beautiful Jekyll

For more dynamic sites: Next.js/React templates; WordPress academic themes (no code); Notion or Carrd (quick, limited).

**Pages to include:** home/about (photo, short bio, interests), publications (PDFs and links by year), projects, CV (downloadable PDF), optional blog, contact.

**Tips:** start from a template; manage citations via Google Scholar; include keywords (area, university) for search; content over aesthetics; update at least quarterly; test on mobile.
